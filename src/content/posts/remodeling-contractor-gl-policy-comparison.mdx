When you're comparing general liability quotes for your remodeling business, the premium is the most visible number — but it's not the most important one. Two quotes with the same limits and similar prices can have dramatically different coverage based on what's in the policy form.

Here's what to look at when you're comparing GL options as a residential remodeling contractor.

## The Occurrence vs. Claims-Made Question

This is the first and most important policy-form question.

An **occurrence policy** covers claims arising from your work done during the policy period, regardless of when the claim is filed. If you finish a project in 2026 and a water damage claim arrives in 2028, your 2026 policy responds — even if you've switched carriers.

A **claims-made policy** covers claims filed while the policy is in force. If you cancel or switch carriers without buying an extended-reporting endorsement (tail coverage), claims filed after cancellation aren't covered — even for work you did while the policy was active.

For residential remodeling with real completed-operations exposure, occurrence coverage is generally preferable. Completed-operations claims often surface 12–24 months after project completion. Claims-made policies require you to maintain continuous coverage or purchase tail coverage every time you switch — an easy gap to miss.

**Red flag:** Some lower-premium GL quotes for contractors are claims-made forms. The cheaper premium may reflect the tail risk being shifted to you.

## The Completed-Operations Aggregate

Standard GL policies have separate aggregate limits for premises/operations and completed operations. The policy might say:

- $1 million per occurrence
- $2 million general aggregate
- $2 million products-completed operations aggregate

That last number is the maximum the policy will pay for all completed-operations claims in a policy period. If your aggregate is $1 million and you have two completed-operations claims in one year — a tile failure and a structural issue — you might exhaust the aggregate on the first claim.

For remodeling contractors doing multiple projects annually, the completed-operations aggregate deserves attention. Compare this number across quotes, not just the per-occurrence limit.

## The Subcontractor Exclusion

Many lower-priced GL policies exclude work performed by subcontractors. The exclusion might read:

*"This insurance does not apply to 'bodily injury' or 'property damage' arising out of operations performed for the Named Insured by independent contractors..."*

For remodeling contractors who use subs for HVAC, plumbing, electrical, tile, or painting, this exclusion can be devastating. If a sub's work causes damage and their own GL is inadequate or lapsed, you may be named in the claim — and if your policy excludes sub work, you have no coverage.

Always ask whether the GL form contains a subcontractor exclusion. If it does, ask whether it can be bought back, and at what cost.

## Classification of Your Operations

GL premiums are calculated based on how your operations are classified — the type of work you do. Remodeling work spans several possible classifications:

- **Residential remodeling contractor** (general)
- **Carpentry — interior**
- **Tile, stone, and marble work**
- **Painting**

The right classification matters because it determines both your premium and whether claims will be covered. If you're classified as an "interior carpentry" contractor but you also do whole-home renovations including structural work, you may have a coverage gap for operations outside the classification.

When reviewing a quote, ask how your business is classified and whether the classification covers everything you do.

## The Pollution Exclusion

Every GL policy has a pollution exclusion. The question is how broad it is and whether you've addressed the gap with CPL.

For residential remodelers working in pre-1978 homes, the pollution exclusion can apply to lead-based paint disturbance, asbestos-containing materials, and potentially mold. If you don't have CPL, these claims are uninsured.

When comparing GL quotes, confirm whether CPL is included or whether you'll need it separately. Some contractor-specific programs include limited pollution coverage; most standard GL forms do not.

## Additional-Insured Endorsements

If you work for GCs or on projects where property owners require additional-insured status, your GL needs to accommodate this. Look for:

- **Blanket additional-insured endorsements** that automatically extend coverage when required by written contract (more convenient than scheduling each AI individually)
- **Primary and non-contributory language** — many GC contracts require that your GL be primary and non-contributory to their policy
- **Additional-insured coverage extended to completed operations** — some AI endorsements only cover ongoing operations, not the completed-operations tail

Ask specifically about these endorsement terms when comparing quotes, especially if you work as a subcontractor.

## Medical Payments

Medical payments coverage pays small medical expenses to third parties injured on your jobsite, regardless of fault — without requiring the injured party to file a liability claim. The typical limit is $5,000 or $10,000 per person.

This isn't a major differentiator between quotes, but having medical payments coverage helps resolve minor injuries quickly and cost-effectively without turning them into liability claims.

## Comparing Quotes Intelligently

When you have multiple GL quotes in front of you:

1. Confirm occurrence vs. claims-made form for each
2. Compare per-occurrence limits, general aggregate, and completed-operations aggregate separately
3. Check for subcontractor exclusions
4. Verify your business classification covers your full scope of work
5. Understand the pollution exclusion and whether CPL is needed
6. Review additional-insured endorsement terms if you work for GCs

Then compare premium relative to the actual coverage provided — not just the stated limits.
